Why do we need a season number?

What it isSome months are busy. Some are slow.

Why it mattersPlan a busy month with slow-month numbers and the target comes out too low.

An ice cream shop sells 100 cones a day in winter and 200 a day in summer. The owner plans summer using the winter average, so the plan says 100. Summer comes and the shop sells 200. The target was too easy.

In our salonDecember is busy. July is slow. The season number is the fix: it says how busy each month usually is.

Deep dive: what counts as sales

Services, treatments and courses, ex VAT, on days the branch traded. Treatments are already inside the Phorest "Services" and "Courses" lines, so nothing is missed. Retail is not in this number. Closed days are left out.

Deep dive: why the latest December is 2025

To find the normal level around a month we need 6 months of sales after it as well as 6 before. December 2026 has not happened yet, so December 2025 is the latest we can test. The method is the same for every month. The live plan in Piece 2 uses Aug, Sep and Oct 2026.

Why it mattersIt is what we divide by. counts half at each end (see deep dive).

Deep dive: why counts half

We want every month of the year to count exactly once. The 13 bars have twice, one at each end. If both counted in full, would be counted twice and pull the normal level towards it. So each end counts half: 0.5 + 0.5 = one . That is also why we divide by 12, not 13.

Piece 2 · One person's plan · Step 1 of 6

What does she sell on a day she comes in?

What you see
For each of her last 3 months: sales ÷ days she came in = her average sale on a day she comes in.

Why it mattersLeave and days off are never counted against her. Only days she worked.

Deep dive: which months count

We always use the last three months before the month we plan (for October: July, August and September). Her sales here include treatments and courses, but not retail. A month counts if she worked 10 days or more. October counts at 6 days or more, because it is not finished. Part-timers count any month with days. Someone who took leave is not marked down: a short month is left out.

Piece 2 · One person's plan · Step 4 of 6

Her days: low, full team and the target

What you seeTwo numbers of days. Her usual days, with the leave we have now, give the low. Her best month, with nobody on leave, gives the full team. The target is the middle.

Why it mattersThe full-team plan is what we could sell with nobody on leave. The low is what we sell today. The target is a goal that still looks real, and the gap between low and full team is what missing staff cost us.

Deep dive: how the days are chosen

Low: the average days she worked in her full months, and the days she came in if she is on leave this month. Full team: her best of the last three months, never more than 26 days. A person who works in two branches gets one plan from both, shared by days. Someone who only covers a few days in a branch has no target there. The most we plan is 26 days. Coach Emma can change a person's days. For December we ask for the roster, because some people then work five days.

Deep dive: the bottom-up check

The steps above are top-down: they only know the past. A bottom-up check starts from the hours instead. Take the hours she can give, count 85% of them as productive (our benchmark), and price them at her average bill per hour. That is what her hours could earn if she runs at 85%. The calculator shows it next to the target, so everyone can see if the number is realistic. If the target needs more than 85% of the hours, it asks too much of them.

An adjustment is a different thing: your own percentage added to or taken off a plan for what the past cannot see (leave, a new starter with a guest book, a part-time rota). Example: a plan that grows 7.3% on her normal day can become 17% for a stylist with a new guest book to fill. The adjustment is written next to the plan so everyone can see it.

Piece 2 · One person's plan · Step 6 of 6

How many clients can her hours hold?

What you seeHer hours a day, the days the target needs, and the utilisation we aim for (85%, anything from 80 to 90 is fine), against how long one client takes her.

Why it mattersA target of clients she has no hours for is a target she cannot reach. If the clients do not fit, the answer is a higher average bill, not more hours.

Deep dive: where the hours come from

Hours a day: the available hours on the utilisation roster for August and September, divided by the days she was rostered. Hours a client: her booked hours divided by her visits in the same two months. Staff whose booked hours are not mostly client time, joiners without a full roster and people in two branches are not held to hours yet.

Together

Put the two pieces together

What you seeThe three boxes from the top, now with real numbers.

Why it mattersHer normal day comes from her sales. The season number comes from the branches' history. One says who she is. The other says what kind of month is coming.

Deep dive: who is treated differently

Joiners (the first 3 months of employment, visa or not) have no history, so they get 70% of what the regulars in the same job plan for, or their own pace if that is lower. Leavers use the months they have (at least two). Someone on leave is planned on the days she came in. Someone who works in two branches has one plan, shared by days, and a branch where she only covers has no target for her. Anyone with fewer than two counted months is checked by Coach Emma first.
